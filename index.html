Our Solar System

The Solar System consists of the Sun, eight planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids, comets and countless smaller objects. It is located inside the Orion Arm of the Milky Way galaxy.

🔭 When & Where Can You See the Planets?

Find out when each planet is generally easiest to observe, where to look in the sky and what equipment you need. The information below uses India as the general reference region. Exact rise and set times vary by city and date.

Important: A planet does not stay in the same place in the sky every night. Earth and the other planets are constantly moving around the Sun. The easiest planets to observe are usually Venus, Jupiter, Mars and Saturn. The best observing opportunities for the outer planets often occur around opposition. For Mercury and Venus, the most useful events are their greatest elongations.

☿ MERCURY

When can you see Mercury?

Mercury is difficult to observe because it never moves very far from the Sun in Earth's sky.

Morning visibility: Look low toward the eastern horizon shortly before sunrise.

Evening visibility: Look low toward the western horizon shortly after sunset.

Best opportunity: Around Mercury's greatest elongations.

DIFFICULT
♀ VENUS

When can you see Venus?

Venus is the brightest planet visible from Earth and is usually extremely easy to identify.

Morning star: Look toward the eastern sky before sunrise.

Evening star: Look toward the western sky after sunset.

Appearance: Very bright white object that normally does not twinkle as strongly as stars.

VERY EASY
🌍 EARTH

Earth — Our Observation Platform

Earth itself cannot normally be viewed as a planet while standing on Earth's surface. Instead, Earth is the platform from which we observe the rest of the Solar System.

OUR HOME
♂ MARS

When can you see Mars?

Mars is easiest to observe when it approaches opposition. At opposition, Earth lies approximately between the Sun and Mars.

Where to look: Along the ecliptic, the general path followed by the Sun and planets across the sky.

Appearance: A relatively bright reddish-orange point.

EASY AROUND OPPOSITION
♃ JUPITER

When can you see Jupiter?

Jupiter is one of the easiest planets to see because it can become extremely bright.

Where to look: Along the ecliptic.

Best period: Around opposition, when Jupiter can remain visible for much of the night.

Equipment: A small telescope can reveal the four large Galilean moons: Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto.

VERY EASY
♄ SATURN

When can you see Saturn?

Saturn is best observed around opposition.

Where to look: Along the ecliptic.

Best feature: Its famous ring system can be seen using a telescope.

During opposition, Saturn rises around sunset and remains visible for much of the night.

EASY
♅ URANUS

When can you see Uranus?

Uranus is much fainter than Jupiter and Saturn. Under exceptionally dark skies it can potentially be detected without optical aid, but binoculars or a telescope make identification much easier.

Best period: Around opposition.

Where: Along the ecliptic near its position among the constellations.

BINOCULARS RECOMMENDED
♆ NEPTUNE

When can you see Neptune?

Neptune is extremely faint and cannot normally be seen with the unaided eye.

Best period: Around opposition.

Equipment: A telescope is required for practical observation.

Where: Along the ecliptic in the region of the sky where Neptune is located at that time.

TELESCOPE REQUIRED

🌠 How to Find a Planet

1. Find the horizon: Choose a place with a clear view of the sky.

2. Avoid city lights: Dark locations make faint planets and stars easier to see.

3. Find the ecliptic: The Sun, Moon and planets appear close to an imaginary line across the sky called the ecliptic.

4. Look for steady objects: Planets generally twinkle much less than stars.

5. Use a sky map: A planetarium application can help identify the exact direction and altitude for your location.

6. Never look directly at the Sun: Viewing the Sun without proper certified solar viewing equipment can permanently damage your eyes.

🌌 Our Galaxy

The Solar System is only a tiny part of a much larger galaxy.

The Milky Way

The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy containing an enormous number of stars. Our Solar System is located in a smaller region of the galaxy called the Orion Arm, also known as the Orion Spur. The Solar System orbits the center of the Milky Way at hundreds of thousands of kilometres per hour and takes roughly 230 million years to complete one orbit around the galactic center.
